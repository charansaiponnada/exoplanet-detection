\begin{abstract}
Transit surveys flag far more events than there are planets, and telling the two
apart is the bottleneck between photometry and a planet catalogue. We present
\phantomnet{}, a vetting network that looks at each candidate through nine
phase-folded views --- global and local flux, odd and even epochs, the secondary
phase, two centroid channels, and folds at the competing period hypotheses
$P/2$ and $2P$ --- combines them with a transformer over per-view tokens, and
fits a five-parameter transit model on the side, from the light curve alone,
without ever being taught a transit shape. On the 15{,}683 labelled Kepler DR24
threshold-crossing events, \phantomnet{} reaches an average precision of
$0.9732 \pm 0.0097$ against $0.9385 \pm 0.0093$ for a faithfully reimplemented
\astronet{} trained on identical splits ($p = 4\times10^{-4}$, five seeds). The
gain is architectural, and we show it by decomposing it: given only the two views
\astronet{} itself receives, \phantomnet{} still wins by $0.0242$
($p = 7\times10^{-3}$), while the extra views and scalars add a smaller,
individually marginal $0.0105$. Operationally, precision at $95$ per cent recall
rises from $0.805 \pm 0.033$ to $0.927 \pm 0.022$ ($p = 2\times10^{-4}$),
cutting the false positives in a follow-up list by $62$ per cent.

We then try to break our own model and report the failures in full. None of the
eight component ablations is statistically significant (the largest single effect
is $0.0072$ in average precision); the star-level split we adopted costs nothing
relative to the event-level split used by earlier work ($p = 0.97$);
gradient-boosted trees on summary statistics match \phantomnet{} on average
precision ($0.9754 \pm 0.0056$) before falling behind at high recall; conformal
$p$-values with Benjamini--Hochberg selection control the false discovery rate of
the candidate list down to about a five per cent target but not below; the
unsupervised decoder recovers transit durations and depths to $3.2$ and $6.5$ per
cent of published values; and zero-shot transfer to $2{,}351$ TESS objects of
interest drops both \phantomnet{} and \astronet{} to an average precision of
$0.79$. We report all of these because each is a result a practitioner relies on,
and each would have been quietly dropped in a paper that published only its wins.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The transit method has produced the large majority of known exoplanets, but the
detections it yields are not planets: they are \emph{threshold-crossing events}
(TCEs), periodic dimmings that survive a matched-filter search of a photometric
time series. Most TCEs are not planetary. They arise from eclipsing binary
stars, from the light of an eclipsing binary blended into the photometric
aperture of an unrelated target, from stellar variability and starspots, and
from instrumental systematics that happen to be periodic. The Kepler mission
alone produced $\sim$$3.4\times10^{4}$ TCEs in its final data release, of which
only a minority correspond to planets \citep{Thompson2018}. Deciding which is
which --- \emph{vetting} --- is the bottleneck between a photometric survey and
a planet catalogue.

Vetting has traditionally been performed by a cascade of physically motivated
diagnostic tests. If odd-numbered and even-numbered transits have different
depths, the signal is probably an eclipsing binary detected at half its true
period. If a shallower dimming appears near the opposite orbital phase, the
companion is self-luminous and therefore stellar. If the flux-weighted centroid
of the target shifts during the event, the light is being removed from a
neighbouring source rather than from the target. If the profile is V-shaped
rather than flat-bottomed, the eclipse is probably grazing. These tests are
fast, interpretable, and were the basis of the Kepler Robovetter
\citep{Thompson2018}. They are also brittle: each is a hand-tuned threshold on a
statistic that degrades rapidly as the signal-to-noise ratio falls, and each
fails in a characteristic way. The odd-even test, in particular, is defeated by
exactly the situation it is meant to catch, because a binary detected at its
half-period alias presents both eclipses as ``primary'' events.

Deep learning changed the picture. \citet{Shallue2018} showed that a
one-dimensional convolutional network applied to two fixed-length
representations of the phase-folded light curve --- a whole-orbit ``global''
view and a zoomed ``local'' view --- outperformed the hand-built cascade, and
used it to recover two previously missed planets. Subsequent work added input
channels rather than changing the reasoning: \citet{Ansdell2018} supplied
centroid time series and stellar parameters, and \exominer{}
\citep{Valizadegan2022} added a branch per diagnostic test and validated 301 new
planets. The same recipe was carried to TESS \citep{Yu2019, Osborn2020}, and more
        recently attention has been introduced over the same two views, together with
        post-hoc temperature scaling, by \citet{Islam2026}. That study is architecturally
        the closest to the present one, so we state the three differences plainly. They
        attend over the two classical views alone; we add views at the competing period
        hypotheses and an explicit comparison between them (Section~\ref{sec:harmonic}).
        They treat calibration as a post-hoc temperature scale; we report calibration but
        rest the operational guarantee on conformal $p$-values instead. And their
        selection guarantee, if any, is not distribution-free; ours is
        (Section~\ref{sec:conformal}).

Two concurrent studies bear directly on the present work. \citet{Priyanshu2026}
give the first application of conformal prediction to transit \emph{detection},
using a self-supervised stellar world model to find single transits in raw flux
and reporting empirical coverage of conformal prediction sets.
\citet{Kopparapu2026} examine cross-instrument prioritisation over all
train/test combinations of Kepler and TESS, and find that a model trained on one
mission transfers poorly to the other, recommending joint training instead. We
return to both in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}: our conformal contribution
targets a different guarantee (false discovery rate over a selected list, rather
than coverage of a prediction set) on a different task (vetting events of known
period, rather than detecting single transits), and our cross-mission experiment
is deliberately zero-shot, which is the setting \citet{Kopparapu2026} identify as
difficult.

Across this line of work the network is given the folded light curve
\emph{at the period reported by the search} and asked to classify what it sees.
That framing embeds an assumption which the classical tests explicitly do not
make: that the reported period is the true period. It is precisely the failure
of that assumption --- the half-period alias --- which produces the dominant
astrophysical false positive. A network shown only the fold at $P$ can learn
proxies for the alias, but it cannot examine the alternative hypothesis
directly, because the evidence that distinguishes $P$ from $2P$ lives in a fold
the network is never shown.

A second gap concerns what the network outputs. Vetting produces a ranked list
that consumes finite follow-up resources, so the operationally meaningful
question is not ``what is the score of this candidate'' but ``if I observe
everything on this list, what fraction of my time is wasted''. A softmax output
does not answer that question: neural network probabilities are systematically
miscalibrated \citep{Guo2017}, and calibration on one data set carries no
guarantee on another. Nor do these classifiers estimate the physical parameters
--- period, depth, duration --- that the survey exists to measure; those are
taken from the upstream search and passed through unchanged.

This paper presents \phantomnet{}, a vetting network built around those two
gaps, and --- more importantly --- a controlled study of which of its parts
actually earn their place. Our contributions are as follows.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A vetting architecture that improves on two-view baselines with
      fewer parameters.} Nine phase-folded views, including folds at the
      competing period hypotheses $P/2$ and $2P$, are encoded independently and
      combined by a transformer over per-view tokens carrying view-type and
      period-hypothesis embeddings (Section~\ref{sec:harmonic}). On the standard
      Kepler DR24 benchmark this improves average precision over a faithfully
      reimplemented \astronet{} by $0.0347$ at matched training budget and
      slightly \emph{lower} parameter count, and cuts the false-positive content
      of a $95$ per cent recall candidate list by $62$ per cent. The gain is
      architectural: restricted to exactly the two views \astronet{} receives,
      \phantomnet{} still beats it by $0.0242$ (Section~\ref{sec:comparison}).
\item \textbf{A differentiable transit decoder.} A five-parameter analytic
      transit model is rendered onto the local view and fitted by reconstruction
      with no parameter supervision (Section~\ref{sec:decoder}). Its bottleneck
      is directly interpretable: the ingress-softness term separates
      flat-bottomed planetary profiles from V-shaped stellar eclipses, and the
      fitted depths and durations agree with published values to $6.5$ and
      $3.2$ per cent respectively without ever seeing a parameter label.
\item \textbf{Distribution-free candidate lists.} Conformal $p$-values computed
      against held-out known non-planets, combined with the
      Benjamini--Hochberg procedure, target a candidate list whose false
      discovery rate is controlled at a chosen level under an exchangeability
      assumption alone (Section~\ref{sec:conformal}). We verify empirically
      where this works and where it fails.
\item \textbf{Two negative results.} We ablate every component over five seeds
      and find that \emph{none} of them individually accounts for the gain: the
      largest single effect is $0.007$ in average precision and no ablation
      reaches significance. We also find that the star-level split we adopted to
      prevent leakage makes no measurable difference relative to the
      event-level split used by earlier work. We report both in full, because a
      field that publishes only the components that worked will keep
      re-discovering the ones that did not.
\end{enumerate}

We regard the second pair as the more useful contribution. Individually
plausible physical priors --- harmonic folds, an explicit alias contrast, a
differentiable transit model, centroid channels --- are each individually
dispensable here, because the evidence they carry is largely redundant with what
the remaining channels already supply. That is a property of the problem rather
than of any one implementation, and it has a direct methodological consequence:
one-component-at-a-time ablation, the standard instrument for attributing credit
in this literature, systematically understates the value of a redundant
ensemble of evidence.

One boundary on our claims should be stated up front. We compare against a
reimplemented \astronet{}, retrained under identical conditions, because it is
the strongest baseline of its class that we can control end to end. The more
elaborate \exominer{} family \citep{Valizadegan2022} --- one branch per
diagnostic test plus catalogue parameters, exactly the design space this paper
extends --- reports higher published accuracy on this benchmark, and we do not
claim to beat it. A head-to-head requires an \exominer{} trained model, which is
not public, and is left to future work. Our contribution is the controlled
decomposition of what a multi-view architecture buys, not a new record on the
benchmark.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{The Kepler DR24 threshold-crossing event set}

We use the Kepler Q1--Q17 DR24 TCE catalogue hosted at the NASA Exoplanet
Archive, which is the benchmark introduced by \citet{Shallue2018} and reused by
\citet{Ansdell2018}. Of its 20{,}367 TCEs, 15{,}737 carry a disposition from the
Autovetter training set \citep{McCauliff2015}:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{PC} (planet candidate, 3{,}600 events) --- the positive class;
\item \textbf{AFP} (astrophysical false positive, 9{,}596 events) --- eclipsing
      binaries, blends and other genuine astrophysical signals that are not
      planetary transits of the target;
\item \textbf{NTP} (non-transiting phenomenon, 2{,}541 events) --- stellar
      variability and instrumental artefacts.
\end{itemize}

These 15{,}737 events lie on 9{,}865 distinct stars; 15{,}683 of them survive
preprocessing (Section~\ref{sec:preprocessing}) and constitute the working set.
Figure~\ref{fig:views} shows the nine input channels for one event of each
class. We treat PC as the positive
class and $\{$AFP, NTP$\}$ as the negative class, following the convention of
the prior work we compare against.

Light curves are the long-cadence ($29.4$~min) PDCSAP photometry from the Kepler
pipeline, retrieved for every quarter in which the target was observed. We
retain cadences whose quality flag contains none of the bits indicating attitude
tweaks, safe modes, coarse or Earth point, desaturation events, manual
exclusions or detector anomalies.

\subsection{The TESS cross-mission set}
\label{sec:tessdata}

To test whether the learned representation is specific to Kepler we assemble a
zero-shot evaluation set from the TESS Objects of Interest catalogue
\citep{Guerrero2021}. We keep TOIs whose TFOPWG disposition is unambiguous ---
confirmed or known planets (CP, KP) as positives, false positives and false
alarms (FP, FA) as negatives --- and which have a published epoch, period and
duration. Photometry is the two-minute-cadence SPOC light curve, limited to the
first four sectors available for each target; only cadences with a zero quality
flag are kept, since the TESS quality bits differ from Kepler's.

\section{From photometry to fixed-length views}
\label{sec:preprocessing}

Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} is the workflow that all four models share, from raw
photometry to the evaluation tables of Section~\ref{sec:results}. Each step is
described below.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig9_pipeline.pdf}
\caption{The evaluation pipeline, identical for every model: quality-flag
masking and gap splitting, B-spline detrending with the transit epochs masked,

star-level 80/10/10 split, training over five seeds, and evaluation.}
\label{fig:pipeline}

\subsection{Detrending}

Stellar variability and residual instrumental trends exceed a planetary transit
in amplitude by orders of magnitude, so each light curve is flattened before
folding. Following \citet{Vanderburg2014} and \citet{Shallue2018} we divide each
contiguous segment --- data separated by gaps longer than $0.75$~d are treated
as separate segments --- by a cubic basis spline fitted with iterative
$3\sigma$ outlier rejection.

Two details matter. First, the knot spacing is not fixed: it is selected per
target from a logarithmic grid spanning $0.5$ to $20$~d by minimising the
Bayesian Information Criterion summed over all segments, so that a rapidly
rotating star and a photometrically quiet one are treated differently. Second,
the cadences within $1.5$ transit durations of \emph{any} TCE on the target are
excluded from the spline fit, though the fitted spline is still evaluated at
those cadences. Without this mask the spline partially absorbs the very signal
being measured, biasing depths low.

The centroid time series are detrended in the same way and with the same knot
spacing, so that only excursions coincident with an event survive.

\subsection{Views}
\label{sec:views}

For each TCE, cadences falling within $1.5$ durations of a \emph{different} TCE
on the same star are removed, and the remainder is folded on the reported
ephemeris. Nine fixed-length views are then rendered by median-binning
(Table~\ref{tab:views}). Each is centred on its median and divided by the
absolute value of its minimum, so that the deepest bin sits at $-1$; the divisor
(hereafter $\kappa$) is retained as a scalar, which is what allows an absolute
depth to be recovered from the normalised view in Section~\ref{sec:decoder}.
All normalisation statistics seen by the network --- medians, minima and
$\kappa$ --- are computed per event and therefore cannot leak split information.

The two harmonic views are the novel channels. Folding at $2P$ maps alternating
eclipses of a binary onto \emph{distinct} phases, so a depth difference that the
odd-even test must infer from two interleaved subsets becomes directly visible
as two separated dimmings of unequal depth. Folding at $P/2$ tests the opposite
hypothesis: if the true period is half the reported one, that fold sharpens the
event instead of smearing it.

The local, odd, even and secondary views span $\pm4$ transit durations. The
secondary view is centred by re-folding about the epoch of the deepest
out-of-primary bin, rather than by windowing inside the primary fold, so that
the window remains fully populated when the secondary lies near phase $\pm0.5$.

\subsection{Scalar features}

Alongside the views the network receives scalar features in four groups:
\emph{transit} (period, duration, depth, model signal-to-noise, multiple event
statistic, number of transits, impact parameter, radius and semi-major axis
ratios), \emph{stellar} (effective temperature, surface gravity, metallicity,
inferred planet radius), \emph{diagnostic} (the pipeline's odd-even and centroid
offset statistics and robust fit statistic), and \emph{derived} --- eleven
quantities measured here from the detrended light curve, including the odd-even
depth asymmetry, the secondary depth ratio, the selected knot spacing, the
out-of-transit scatter, the normalisation scale, and a red-noise proxy formed
from the ratio of the scatter of six-hour bins to the white-noise expectation.
The derived group is the only one computable identically for both missions and
is therefore the configuration used for the cross-mission experiment.
Features spanning orders of magnitude are log-compressed, missing values are
imputed to the training-split median, and all are standardised with
training-split statistics.

\section{The \phantomnet{} architecture}
\label{sec:model}

\phantomnet{} has three parts: a per-view convolutional encoder, a harmonic
attention block over the resulting tokens, and a differentiable transit decoder.
Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} is the map of the whole network.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{figures/fig8_architecture.pdf}
\caption{The \phantomnet{} architecture. Nine views are encoded independently
by residual CNNs into 256-dimensional tokens; view-type and period-hypothesis
embeddings and a classification token are combined by a three-layer
transformer. The harmonic contrast head pools the tokens by period hypothesis
and passes their differences (Eq.~\ref{eq:contrast}) to the classifier, while a
differentiable five-parameter transit model is rendered onto the local view and
fitted by reconstruction alone. Outputs: a vetting score, five physical
parameters, and a reconstruction residual.}
\label{fig:architecture}
\end{figure}

\subsection{View encoders}

Each view is encoded by a stack of pre-activation residual blocks with
GroupNorm, interleaved with strided max-pooling --- five stages for the 2001-bin
views and three for the 201-bin views. The final feature map is reduced by
concatenated mean and max pooling, capturing respectively the average level and
the depth of the deepest excursion, and projected to a token of dimension
$d=256$. Views of equal length share an architecture but not weights, since a
centroid view and a flux view carry different meaning.

\subsection{Harmonic-hypothesis attention}
\label{sec:harmonic}

Each token receives two additive embeddings: a view-type embedding and a
\emph{period-hypothesis} embedding drawn from $\{P/2,\,P,\,2P\}$. A learnable
classification token is prepended and the sequence is processed by a
pre-norm transformer encoder \citep{Vaswani2017} of three layers and eight
heads.

Attention alone leaves the harmonic comparison implicit, so we add an explicit
contrast. Writing $h_{P/2}$, $h_{P}$ and $h_{2P}$ for the mean of the encoded
tokens under each hypothesis, the contrast vector
\begin{equation}
c = \big[\,h_{P}\ \|\ h_{P}-h_{P/2}\ \|\ h_{P}-h_{2P}\ \|\ |h_{P}-h_{2P}|\,\big]
\label{eq:contrast}
\end{equation}
is passed through a single hidden layer and concatenated into the classifier
input. The difference terms state directly whether the evidence prefers the
reported period over its harmonics; the absolute-value term makes the comparison
insensitive to the sign of the discrepancy, which is what the eclipsing-binary
hypothesis requires.

\subsection{Differentiable transit decoder}
\label{sec:decoder}

Let $x$ index the local-view grid in units of transit durations from
mid-transit. From the encoded local-view token an affine head predicts five
bounded parameters: depth $\delta>0$, half-duration $T\in(0.05,2)$, phase offset
$x_0\in(-0.5,0.5)$, limb-darkening strength $u\in(0,1)$ and ingress softness
$s\in(0.02,1)$. With $z = |x-x_0|/T$ the model light curve is
\begin{equation}
\hat{f}(x) = -\,\delta\;
  \underbrace{\sigma\!\left(\frac{1-z}{s}\right)}_{\text{in-transit indicator}}\;
  \underbrace{\Big[1-u\big(1-\sqrt{\max(1-z^{2},0)}\big)\Big]}_{\text{limb darkening}},
\label{eq:render}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ is the logistic function. Equation~\ref{eq:render} is analytic and
differentiable in all five parameters, so it can be trained by backpropagation
through a reconstruction loss against the observed local view.

The softness $s$ is the physically interesting parameter. As $s\to0$ the
indicator approaches a step and the profile becomes flat-bottomed, the signature
of a small opaque body crossing the stellar disc. As $s$ grows the ingress and
egress ramps broaden until the profile is V-shaped, the signature of a grazing
or blended stellar eclipse. A single scalar therefore expresses the U-versus-V
distinction that the classical shape test tries to capture with a ratio of
widths, but it is fitted jointly with depth, duration and limb darkening rather
than measured on a noisy curve in isolation.

Two quantities derived from the fit enter the classifier: the root-mean-square
reconstruction residual and its maximum absolute value. A genuine transit is
well described by Equation~\ref{eq:render}; a stellar eclipse or an instrumental
artefact is not, and the residual records that mismatch.

Critically, the five parameters are \emph{not} regressed against catalogue
values. Supervising them would teach the network to reproduce the pipeline fit
against which it is later compared, making any parameter-recovery result
circular. They are identified by reconstruction alone.

\subsection{Objective}

The training objective is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BCE}}(\hat{y}, y)
  \;+\; \lambda\,\big\| \hat{f} - f_{\mathrm{local}} \big\|_2^2 ,
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
with $\lambda=5$. Networks are optimised with AdamW \citep{Loshchilov2019} under
a one-cycle schedule, batch size 128, gradient clipping at norm 5, and early
stopping on validation average precision. Views are randomly mirrored about
mid-transit during training, which is a valid symmetry of a transit profile.

\section{Evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

\subsection{Splits}
\label{sec:splits}

Our primary protocol splits $80/10/10$ into training, validation and test sets
\emph{by star}: every TCE on a given target falls in exactly one fold. Multiple
TCEs on one star share a light curve, the same detrending solution, the same
stellar parameters and the same instrumental systematics, so a TCE-level split
places near-duplicates on both sides of the boundary and inflates test scores.
Because previous work reports the TCE-level split, we also report it, so that
the comparison to published numbers is like-for-like and the size of the leakage
is itself measurable.

\subsection{Metrics and conventions}
\label{sec:conventions}

We report the area under the ROC curve, average precision (the area under the
precision-recall curve), and precision at fixed recall. The last is the
operational quantity: a vetting pipeline is run at high recall so as not to
discard planets, and is then judged by how much follow-up effort the surviving
false positives consume.

Throughout, catalogue figures are seed means of \emph{single} networks: every
entry in Tables~\ref{tab:main}, \ref{tab:ablation} and \ref{tab:conformal} is the
mean over five random seeds of a single-run model, quoted with the standard
deviation over those seeds, and every significance test (Welch's $t$-test
\citep{Welch1947} on the per-seed values) is computed on those single-run
values. The fixed five-member ensemble built on the validation split is used
\emph{only} where stable scores are required --- the decoder parameter
uncertainties of Section~\ref{sec:protocol}, the calibration diagnostics and the
conformal selection of Section~\ref{sec:conformal} --- and is never quoted in the
headline comparisons. This keeps the reported means and the significance tests on
the same object.

\subsection{Distribution-free candidate selection}
\label{sec:conformal}

Let $s$ denote the model score. Treating ``this event is not a planet'' as the
null hypothesis, and given $n$ scores $s_1,\dots,s_n$ of \emph{known
non-planets} from the held-out validation split, the conformal $p$-value of a
test event with score $s$ is
\begin{equation}
p = \frac{1 + \#\{i : s_i \ge s\}}{n+1} .
\label{eq:pvalue}
\end{equation}
Applying the Benjamini--Hochberg procedure \citep{Benjamini1995} at level $q$ to
these $p$-values yields a candidate list whose false discovery rate is
controlled at $q$ \citep{Bates2023}. The guarantee is finite-sample and
distribution-free: it requires only that calibration and test events be
exchangeable, and holds regardless of whether the network is well specified or
well calibrated. For comparison we also report the expected calibration error
before and after temperature scaling \citep{Guo2017}.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{figures/fig10_candidate_selection.pdf}
\caption{Distribution-free candidate selection. Each score is converted to a
conformal $p$-value against the held-out known non-planets
($n_{\mathrm{cal}} \approx 1{,}200$). A Benjamini--Hochberg threshold at level
$q$ either admits the event to the candidate list, whose false discovery rate is
then controlled at $q$, or rejects it as unobserved, consuming no follow-up
time.}
\label{fig:candidate}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Transit parameter recovery}

The decoder bottleneck is converted to physical units by
$\delta_{\mathrm{ppm}} = \delta\,\kappa\times10^{6}$, where $\kappa$ is the
normalisation scale divided out of the local view, and
$T_{\mathrm{dur}} = 2T\,T_{\mathrm{cat}}$, where $T_{\mathrm{cat}}$ is the
catalogue duration defining the grid. Uncertainties are the standard deviation
across ensemble members \citep{Lakshminarayanan2017}.

These estimates are compared against transit parameters from the NASA Exoplanet
Archive's composite planetary parameters table --- that is, against published
follow-up analyses rather than against the Kepler pipeline whose fit is being
assessed. The comparison is therefore between two independent estimates of the
same physical quantity: the pipeline value that seeded the search, and the value
recovered here.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

All numbers are test-set values on the star-level split, averaged over five
random seeds and quoted with their standard deviation; significance is assessed
with Welch's $t$-test on the per-seed values.

\subsection{Comparison with baselines}
\label{sec:comparison}

Table~\ref{tab:main} gives the headline comparison. \phantomnet{} improves
average precision over \astronet{} by $0.0347$ ($t = 5.75$, $p = 4.3\times
10^{-4}$) while using slightly fewer parameters ($9.16$M against $10.55$M), so
the difference is attributable to the architecture and the input channels rather
than to capacity. The reimplemented \astronet{} reaches AUC $0.9825$ here; the
originally published value is $0.988$, and the small shortfall is expected given
that our preprocessing chain, quality masking and split protocol differ in
detail from the original.

The operationally meaningful comparison is the last column. A vetting pipeline
is run at high recall so as not to discard planets, and is then judged by the
follow-up effort its false positives consume. At $95$ per cent recall the
\astronet{} candidate list is $19.5$ per cent false positives and the
\phantomnet{} list is $7.3$ per cent, a reduction of $62$ per cent in wasted
effort, and the $0.927\pm0.022$ versus $0.805\pm0.033$ gap is significant at
five seeds ($p = 2\times10^{-4}$). Against the tree the advantage is smaller
($0.927 \pm 0.022$ versus $0.901 \pm 0.034$) and is not significant in this
protocol ($p = 0.20$). Figure~\ref{fig:prroc} shows the full precision-recall
and ROC curves.

\textbf{Why the model wins.} Table~\ref{tab:ablation} lets us separate the two
factors --- architecture and input channels --- that Table~\ref{tab:main} conflates.
Restricted to the global and local views alone, the two channels \astronet{}
itself receives, \phantomnet{} reaches $0.9627 \pm 0.0117$ against \astronet{}'s
$0.9385 \pm 0.0093$: a gain of $0.0242$ from the transformer-over-views
architecture on identical inputs ($p = 7\times10^{-3}$). Adding back the other
seven views and the scalars lifts the full model to $0.9732$, an increment of
$0.0105$ that is individually marginal ($p = 0.16$). The headline gap is
therefore mostly an architectural one, and the multi-channel contribution ---
clearly real, but smaller and not individually significant --- is best read as
redundant insurance that helps most in particular failure modes, as the
ablations below show.

Two baseline results deserve emphasis. The classical cascade --- the odd-even,
secondary-eclipse and V-shape tests, implemented carefully and with the
look-elsewhere correction that the secondary test requires --- reaches an
average precision of only $0.40$. Its individual tests are nonetheless sharply
diagnostic: the corrected secondary test fires on $34$ per cent of astrophysical
false positives and $1$ per cent of planet candidates. The gap between sharp
individual tests and poor aggregate performance is precisely the gap that
learned combination closes.

More surprisingly, gradient-boosted trees on the scalar feature vector match
\phantomnet{} on average precision ($0.9754$ versus $0.9732$, $p = 0.67$) and
beat \astronet{} comfortably, despite never seeing a light curve. The scalar
vector includes the pipeline's own data-validation diagnostics, which encode a
great deal of the vetting problem already. The tree nevertheless falls behind at
high recall ($0.902$ against $0.927$ at $95$ per cent recall), which is where a
survey operates. Any claim that a deep model is necessary for this task must
clear this baseline, and we would encourage its inclusion in future work.

\subsection{The split protocol makes no difference}
\label{sec:splitresult}

We adopted a star-level split on the expectation that multiple TCEs on one star
--- sharing a light curve, a detrending solution, stellar parameters and
systematics --- would leak between folds under the event-level split used by
earlier work. They do not. \phantomnet{} scores $0.9732 \pm 0.0097$ under the
star-level split and $0.9734 \pm 0.0059$ under the event-level split
($p = 0.97$); \astronet{} scores $0.9385$ and $0.9315$ ($p = 0.19$). The effect
is consistent with zero for both models.

This is not because sharing is rare. $65.1$ per cent of the labelled events sit
on a star carrying at least one other labelled event, and $44.3$ per cent of the
stars host more than one. The explanation must therefore be that sibling events
are not near-duplicates of each other. They are typically dissimilar signals ---
a genuine transit alongside a systematic at an unrelated period, with different
depths, durations and shapes --- so knowing the label of one conveys little
about the other beyond the shared stellar and instrumental context, which is
apparently not what the classifier keys on.

We continue to recommend the star-level split on principle, since its cost is
nil and the argument for it does not depend on the size of the effect. But we
can report that published results using the event-level split are not measurably
inflated by it, and that the leakage concern, at least on this benchmark, is
unfounded.

The pattern is nonetheless orderly, and it should be read carefully. The rows
of Table~\ref{tab:ablation} are alternative configurations, not a controlled
chain of successive deletions, so we do not claim a monotone degradation. What
the table does show is twofold. First, every single-component deletion is null:
none of the eight tests reaches significance, and none is close. Even
uncorrected --- and with eight tests at $\alpha = 0.05$ any correction only
strengthens the conclusion --- the largest $p$-value difference is $+0.0121$
($p = 0.21$). Second, the restricted configurations degrade more than any single
deletion: dropping the harmonic views, the centroid views or the catalogue
diagnostics one at a time costs $0.0030$-$0.0072$, but withdrawing all the extra
channels together costs $0.0104$, and removing all scalars as well costs $0.0121$.
The components are individually dispensable and collectively load-bearing, which
is the signature of redundant evidence. A grazing eclipsing binary, for instance,
betrays itself in the V-shaped local profile, in the decoder's ingress-softness
parameter, in the odd-even depth ratio and in the $2P$ fold; deleting any one
leaves the other three.

What resolution does this protocol actually have? With the observed seed spreads
($0.006$-$0.017$ in average precision), a two-sided Welch test at five seeds and
$80$ per cent power detects differences of roughly $0.02$ when the spread is
typical ($0.010$) and $0.03$ when it is largest ($0.017$). The nulls must
therefore be read as upper bounds, not as proofs of worthlessness: they exclude
individual contributions above about one to three points of average precision,
and they cannot, by construction, detect the $\sim$$0.001$-$0.006$ effects shown
here. The comparison that does carry information is the architectural contrast
drawn in Section~\ref{sec:comparison} --- same inputs, different combination ---
not any single deletion.

\paragraph{A controlled nested chain.} Table~\ref{tab:ablation} varies one
component against the full model, which is the right design for asking whether
any single component is load-bearing but the wrong one for asking how evidence
degrades as it is withdrawn cumulatively, since each row is compared against a
different alternative rather than against the row before it. We therefore ran a
second, strictly nested sequence: starting from the full model, each
configuration is a subset of its predecessor's inputs, ending at the two views
\astronet{} itself receives (Table~\ref{tab:nested}). Five seeds per link, as
before.

The chain is not monotone at every step --- removing the harmonic views after
the centroid views has already been removed leaves AP essentially unchanged
($-0.0024$, within noise) --- but the cumulative pattern is exactly what
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} implied without being able to show directly: evidence
degrades in one large jump and several small ones, not smoothly. Losing the
scalar block costs $0.0121$, all of it non-significant one component at a time.
Losing the centroid views on top of that costs a further $0.0268$
($p = 2\times10^{-3}$), which is the single largest effect anywhere in this
study and is significant even though the identical deletion, made against the
full model with scalars still present, was not (Table~\ref{tab:ablation},
$+0.0072$, $p = 0.31$). The centroid views and the scalar diagnostics are
therefore substitutes for each other: the pipeline's own centroid features
(\texttt{tce\_dikco\_msky}, \texttt{tce\_dicco\_msky} --- the top-ranked GBDT
feature in Section~\ref{sec:comparison}) and the raw centroid views encode
overlapping information about photocenter motion, and the model needs only one
of the two. The last three links, which converge on \astronet{}'s own two-view
input set, add little beyond that: by the time centroid and scalar evidence is
gone, dropping the harmonic folds, the secondary view, and finally the odd/even
views costs $0.0019$--$0.0027$ each, small individually but cumulatively enough
to land within $0.0035$ of \astronet{}'s own reported operating point on
identical inputs. That gap --- architecture holding inputs fixed at both ends of
the chain --- is the cleanest single number in this study for how much of the
headline improvement is attributable to the transformer fusion itself rather
than to the extra channels: on \astronet{}'s two views, \phantomnet{} still wins
by $0.0242$ (Section~\ref{sec:conclusions}), and the remaining seven views and
scalar block recover most, but not all, of the difference between that number
and the $0.0347$ gap in Table~\ref{tab:ablation}.

\subsection{Calibration and distribution-free candidate selection}
\label{sec:conformalresult}

The conformal selection, the calibration diagnostics and the decoder
uncertainties are computed on the fixed five-member ensemble described in
Section~\ref{sec:conventions}; temperature scaling reduces its expected
calibration error from $0.0142$ to $0.0120$ ($T = 1.12$). The network is
already close to calibrated before scaling, which is not typical of deep
classifiers \citep{Guo2017} and which we attribute to the modest capacity and
strong augmentation.

Calibration, however, is a statement about average agreement, not a guarantee.
Table~\ref{tab:conformal} reports the realised false discovery rate of the
conformal selection of Section~\ref{sec:conformal} against its nominal target.
For targets of $5$ per cent and above the procedure behaves as advertised: at
$q=0.10$ it selects $389$ candidates with a realised FDR of $0.090$ and $96.7$
per cent recall, and at $q = 0.20$ it realises $0.194$. Below $5$ per cent it
does not: at $q = 0.01$ the realised rate is $0.033$, more than three times
nominal.

The failure is expected rather than anomalous, and has two causes. The

Benjamini--Hochberg controls the false discovery rate under such dependence only
asymptotically unless the conditional-calibration variant of \citet{Bates2023} is
used. Practitioners wanting guarantees at the one-per-cent level should enlarge
the calibration set or adopt that variant; at the five-to-twenty per cent level
that a follow-up programme actually operates in, the simple procedure is
adequate.

\subsection{Zero-shot transfer to TESS}
\label{sec:tessresult}

Applied without any adaptation to $2{,}351$ TESS objects of interest
($1{,}298$ confirmed or known planets, $1{,}053$ false positives or false
alarms), both networks degrade sharply: \phantomnet{} reaches an average
precision of $0.789$ (AUC $0.751$) and \astronet{} $0.797$ (AUC $0.767$),
against a chance rate of $0.552$. Both retain real discriminative power, and
neither is usable as a TESS vetter at Kepler-level accuracy.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{figures/fig7_cross_mission.pdf}
\caption{Zero-shot transfer to 2{,}351 labelled TESS objects of interest. Both
architectures retain discriminative power well above the chance rate but are far
from their Kepler accuracy, and are indistinguishable from one another.}
\label{fig:tess}
\end{figure}

The two models are statistically indistinguishable here, so the architectural
advantage established on Kepler does not survive the mission transfer. This
corroborates \citet{Kopparapu2026}, who report that single-mission training
transfers poorly across instruments and recommend joint training. The causes are
not mysterious: TESS light curves are two-minute rather than $29.4$-minute
cadence, span four sectors rather than four years, have a pixel scale an order
of magnitude coarser, and the TOI population is dominated by shorter-period,
brighter-host systems than the Kepler TCE population. We report the result as a
bound on what zero-shot transfer buys, not as a negative verdict on either
architecture.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What this study does and does not establish}

The reliable finding is a comparative one: on a fixed benchmark, with matched
training budget and identical splits, a multi-channel architecture with per-view
tokens beats the standard two-view convolutional baseline by a margin that is
large in operational terms --- most of a factor of three in wasted follow-up at
fixed recall --- and does so with fewer parameters. That result is stable across
seeds and split protocols.

What the study does not establish is \emph{why}, at the level of individual
components. We set out to demonstrate that explicit harmonic reasoning would
resolve the half-period alias that defeats the classical odd-even test, and
built both the harmonic views and a contrast head (Eq.~\ref{eq:contrast}) to
that end. Neither survives ablation: the contrast head is worth
$-0.0007 \pm$ noise, and the harmonic views $+0.0030$. We report this because the
alternative --- quietly dropping the ablation and keeping the mechanism in the
architecture diagram --- is how unfalsifiable folklore accumulates in an applied
field.

The honest reading is that the harmonic evidence is real but redundant. A binary
detected at its half-period alias is visible in the $2P$ fold, but it is also
visible in the odd-even depth ratio, in the secondary-phase view and in the
decoder's shape parameter. Supplying it a fourth time adds little. This is
consistent with the restricted-configuration degradation of
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} and with the fact that the full gap to \astronet{}
only appears once the architecture changes as well.

\subsection{The strength of the tabular baseline}

That gradient-boosted trees on summary statistics match a deep multi-channel
network on average precision is, we think, the most practically important
observation here. The Kepler data-validation products already encode much of
what vetting requires, and a strong tabular learner extracts it cheaply and
interpretably. The deep model earns its keep specifically in the high-recall
regime and in settings where those diagnostics are unavailable --- which
includes, notably, any survey without a mature data-validation pipeline behind
it. Reporting a deep model against only \astronet{} and the classical cascade,
as is common, overstates the case for depth.

\subsection{Limitations}

Our labels are Autovetter dispositions \citep{McCauliff2015}, themselves the
output of an automated classifier, so the ceiling reported here is agreement
with that labelling rather than with ground truth; the PC class in particular
contains an unknown fraction of unconfirmed candidates. The comparison to
\astronet{} is to our reimplementation on our preprocessing chain, not to the
original authors' trained model. The conformal analysis assumes exchangeability
between validation and test events, which the star-level split makes plausible
but does not guarantee, and it is calibrated on a set of $\sim$$1{,}200$
negatives, which as shown bounds the smallest usable target rate. The TESS
experiment uses at most four sectors per target and TFOPWG dispositions that are
themselves provisional. Finally, five seeds bound our resolution: at $80$ per
cent power the protocol detects differences of roughly $0.02$-$0.03$ in average
precision (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}), so the null ablations are upper bounds
that exclude moderate-to-large effects, not small ones.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

We presented \phantomnet{}, a transit-vetting network that combines nine
phase-folded views --- including folds at competing period hypotheses --- through
a transformer over per-view tokens, together with a differentiable transit model
fitted by reconstruction alone. On the Kepler DR24 benchmark it improves average
precision over a matched \astronet{} baseline from $0.9385$ to $0.9732$ and cuts
the false-positive content of a $95$ per cent recall candidate list by $62$ per
cent, using fewer parameters. Most of that gain is architectural rather than a
matter of input channels: restricted to the two views \astronet{} itself
receives, \phantomnet{} already wins by $0.0242$, and the remaining seven views
and the scalar features add a smaller, individually marginal $0.0105$. Conformal
$p$-values with Benjamini--Hochberg
selection deliver false-discovery-rate control at the five-to-twenty per cent
targets a follow-up programme operates in, and the unsupervised decoder recovers
transit durations and depths to a few per cent of published values while
providing an interpretable U-versus-V shape discriminant.

We also report what did not work. No individual component of the architecture is
statistically responsible for its advantage; the star-level split we adopted to
prevent leakage makes no measurable difference; gradient-boosted trees on
summary statistics match the network on average precision; conformal control
fails below a five per cent target; and zero-shot transfer to TESS degrades both
architectures to indistinguishable performance. Each of these is a result a
practitioner benefits from knowing, and none of them would have appeared in a
paper reporting only its successes.

The broader methodological point concerns attribution under redundancy. Vetting
is a problem in which several physically distinct diagnostics point at the same
false positives, and in which the standard leave-one-out ablation therefore
reports that nothing matters while the ensemble plainly does. Future work on
architectures for this task should report nested ablation sequences rather than
single deletions, and should include a strong tabular baseline. We release the
full pipeline --- acquisition, preprocessing, training, evaluation and figure
generation --- to make both straightforward.
